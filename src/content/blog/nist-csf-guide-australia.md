## What NIST CSF actually is

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework, now in its 2.0 revision (February 2024), is a voluntary framework published by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology. It organises cyber risk management into six functions:

  <table style="width:100%;border-collapse:collapse;font-size:0.96rem">
    <thead style="background:#f3f4f6">
      <tr><th style="text-align:left;padding:10px;border-bottom:2px solid #e5e7eb">Function</th><th style="text-align:left;padding:10px;border-bottom:2px solid #e5e7eb">What it covers</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb"><strong>Govern</strong></td><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb">New in 2.0 — strategy, roles, policy and oversight of the whole program. Everything else sits underneath it.</td></tr>
      <tr><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb"><strong>Identify</strong></td><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb">Asset inventory, risk assessment, understanding what you're actually protecting.</td></tr>
      <tr><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb"><strong>Protect</strong></td><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb">Access control, awareness training, data security, protective technology.</td></tr>
      <tr><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb"><strong>Detect</strong></td><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb">Continuous monitoring, anomaly detection.</td></tr>
      <tr><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb"><strong>Respond</strong></td><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb">Incident response planning, communication, mitigation.</td></tr>
      <tr><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb"><strong>Recover</strong></td><td style="padding:8px 10px;border-bottom:1px solid #e5e7eb">Restoration planning, resilience, lessons learned.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

**The part that trips people up:** there is no accredited body that certifies an organisation "NIST CSF compliant," the way SGS or BSI certifies you to ISO 27001. NIST CSF works by comparing a **Current Profile** (where your controls actually are) against a **Target Profile** (where you want them), scored against four **Implementation Tiers** — Partial, Risk-Informed, Repeatable, and Adaptive. It's a self-assessed common language for describing cyber maturity, not a pass/fail credential.

If someone asks you for "NIST CSF certification," the accurate answer is that it doesn't exist — what they usually mean is a documented Current Profile and a credible plan to close the gaps to a Target Profile.

None of that makes NIST CSF mandatory in Australia. It's worth taking seriously when one of those situations actually applies to you, and safe to deprioritise when none of them do.

## The part that actually matters: it maps onto what you already have

The reason NIST CSF is worth understanding even if you never formally adopt it is that its six functions describe the same underlying reality as ISO 27001's Annex A controls and the Essential Eight's mitigation strategies — just organised differently and given different names.

<div style="background:#eff6ff;border-left:4px solid #1e40af;border-radius:0 8px 8px 0;padding:16px 20px;margin:24px 0;">
<strong style="display:block;font-size:0.75rem;text-transform:uppercase;letter-spacing:1px;color:#1e40af;margin-bottom:8px;">In practice</strong>
An organisation already running ISO 27001 or Essential Eight has already done most of what a NIST CSF Current Profile would document — access control, patching, backups, incident response, logging. Producing a NIST CSF profile from that position is a re-labelling exercise, not a rebuild. Starting from nothing is a genuinely different amount of work.
